# Civizen Governance Framework

**Status: Working Governance Framework**

**Working Draft 0.1**

This document is a **working governance framework** and an authoritative project reference. It describes proposed rules for distributing and exercising authority. It does **not** assert that the named boards, councils, constitutions, reserved-matter procedures, or multi-body approval mechanisms already legally exist or are operational in production.

Proposed bodies named here remain **proposed** until they are established through valid legal formation, governing documents, and applicable law.

This Governance Framework is **subordinate to** the [Civizen Institutional Blueprint](./institutional-blueprint.md). The two documents must not be merged.

## 1. Purpose

The Civizen Governance Framework defines how authority is distributed, exercised, constrained, reviewed, and progressively transferred throughout the Civizen system.

The [Institutional Blueprint](./institutional-blueprint.md) defines the major institutional bodies and relationships within Civizen.

This Governance Framework defines:

- who may propose decisions;
- who may advise;
- who may deliberate;
- who may vote;
- who may approve;
- who may implement;
- who may review or audit;
- who may appeal;
- which decisions require specialized expertise;
- which decisions require broad citizen participation;
- which matters remain legally reserved to fiduciary bodies;
- which powers no participant may exercise alone;
- and how governance should evolve as Civizen matures.

The goal is not to create a single centralized decision-making institution.

The goal is to create a governance system in which authority follows **legitimate responsibility, competence, affected interests, contribution, accountability, and constitutional safeguards**.

No individual, organization, government, investor, political movement, technology provider, or automated system should be capable of acquiring unilateral control over Civizen.

## 2. Governance Principles

### 2.1 Authority Must Be Distributed

Civizen should not concentrate governance authority in a single person or body.

Different types of decisions should require different combinations of:

- citizens;
- contributors;
- experts;
- institutional partners;
- governing boards;
- affected communities;
- implementation teams;
- and independent reviewers.

### 2.2 Authority Follows the Decision

There should not be one voting mechanism for every Civizen decision.

Authority should depend on the nature of the matter being decided.

A technical decision may require substantial expert authority.

A constitutional decision may require broad system-wide participation.

A legally regulated matter may require formal fiduciary approval.

A local implementation decision may primarily belong to the affected community and implementation partners.

### 2.3 Participation Does Not Equal Unlimited Authority

Participation in Civizen should create opportunities to contribute to governance, but participation alone should not create authority over every subject.

Governance rights may depend on:

- relevance;
- expertise;
- contribution;
- demonstrated responsibility;
- affected status;
- role;
- institutional mandate;
- and the type of decision.

### 2.4 Money Does Not Purchase Mission Control

Financial contribution should not automatically produce governance power over Civizen's mission.

Investors, donors, governments, foundations, institutional partners, and other funders may receive rights appropriate to contracts, corporate structures, grants, or funded programs.

Those rights must not automatically extend to control over Civizen's constitutional principles, shared mission, or system-wide governance.

### 2.5 Contribution Does Not Automatically Equal Governance Authority

Civizen should record and recognize meaningful contribution.

Contribution may affect:

- reputation;
- recognition;
- contributor status;
- eligibility for greater responsibility;
- economic participation;
- compensation;
- or access to governance roles.

However:

**Contribution ≠ automatic governance authority.**

Likewise:

**Capital contribution ≠ work contribution ≠ institutional contribution ≠ expertise ≠ governance authority.**

Each can be recognized appropriately without treating them as interchangeable.

The persistent **Contribution Record** described in the [Institutional Blueprint](./institutional-blueprint.md) (§13.2) is the institutional mechanism for that history. Contribution history may support recognition, responsibility eligibility, contributor status, compensation, proceeds participation, and appropriate governance eligibility. A single numeric contribution score must not directly determine voting power.

### 2.6 Expertise Matters

Civizen should not reduce expert-dependent matters to unrestricted popularity voting.

Subject-matter expertise should have meaningful weight where incorrect decisions could create substantial harm.

At the same time, expertise should not become permanent technocratic control over decisions that legitimately belong to society.

### 2.7 Affected People Must Be Represented

People and communities materially affected by a decision should normally have meaningful participation in that decision.

### 2.8 Legal Responsibility Must Remain Real

Legal entities operating within Civizen must comply with their actual legal and fiduciary responsibilities.

Community governance cannot require directors or officers to perform unlawful acts or violate fiduciary obligations.

Legal responsibility should not, however, become a permanent justification for overriding Civizen's participatory governance.

### 2.9 AI Advises, Humans Govern

Artificial intelligence may assist Civizen's governance extensively.

AI may not become Civizen's sovereign authority.

AI should not independently:

- enact constitutional changes;
- determine rights;
- remove governance participants;
- award itself authority;
- allocate fundamental political power;
- or make irreversible high-impact governance decisions without accountable human authorization.

### 2.10 Governance Should Become More Distributed Over Time

During Civizen's formative stage, governance will necessarily be more concentrated.

As institutional maturity, participation, security, knowledge, and accountability increase, authority should progressively move toward broader distributed governance.

## 3. Governance Participants

Civizen governance may involve the following principal participants.

### 3.1 Founder

The founder provides early institutional leadership, conceptual continuity, and initial stewardship.

Founder authority should be substantial enough to protect the integrity of Civizen during formation, but explicitly transitional.

### 3.2 Civizen Foundation Board

The Foundation Board exercises the fiduciary authority legally required of the mission-holding institution.

It protects the mission, assets, institutional integrity, and lawful operation of the Foundation.

### 3.3 Citizens

Citizens are participants in the Civizen system who may participate in deliberation and governance according to defined eligibility rules.

### 3.4 Contributors

Contributors are individuals or organizations whose meaningful work, knowledge, resources, implementation, research, infrastructure, or other contribution has been recorded by Civizen.

Contributor status may qualify participants for additional governance responsibilities where relevant.

### 3.5 Expert Councils

Expert Councils provide specialized knowledge, evaluation, standards development, review, certification, or recommendations within defined subject areas.

### 3.6 Institutional Partners

Universities, governments, nonprofits, international organizations, companies, research institutions, communities, and other partners may participate in governance related to their legitimate institutional role.

### 3.7 Affected Communities

People substantially affected by a proposed decision should receive meaningful participation rights even when they are not otherwise highly active Civizen contributors.

### 3.8 Implementation Teams

Teams responsible for delivering approved programs should receive reasonable operational authority within approved mandates.

### 3.9 Commercial Entities

Commercial entities operating within the Civizen ecosystem may govern their lawful commercial affairs, but should not control Civizen's public-interest mission.

### 3.10 Investors

Investors may receive economic and corporate rights appropriate to a commercial entity.

Investment should not create authority over Civizen's constitutional mission.

### 3.11 Governments and Public Authorities

Governments retain legitimate authority within their lawful jurisdictions.

Participation in Civizen should not provide any government with unilateral control over the Civizen system.

### 3.12 Artificial Intelligence

AI is an analytical, advisory, facilitative, monitoring, and implementation-support layer.

It is not an independent sovereign governance participant.

## 4. Distribution of Authority

Civizen governance should distribute authority across several functions.

The principal governance functions are:

1. **Propose**
2. **Advise**
3. **Deliberate**
4. **Vote**
5. **Approve**
6. **Implement**
7. **Review**
8. **Audit**
9. **Appeal**
10. **Suspend or intervene in exceptional circumstances**

These functions should not normally belong to the same participant in major decisions.

## 5. Preliminary Authority Matrix

The following matrix establishes the general direction of authority.

It is not intended to replace detailed decision-specific rules.

| Participant | Propose | Advise | Vote | Approve | Implement | Review / Audit | Unilateral System Control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Founder | Yes | Yes | Transitional | Transitional / limited | Yes | No exclusive authority | No |
| Foundation Board | Yes | Yes | Yes | Fiduciary and reserved matters | Through authorized teams | Oversight | No |
| Citizens | Yes | Yes | Yes where eligible | Defined public matters | Participate | Transparency review | No |
| Contributors | Yes | Yes | Yes where eligible | Defined contributor matters | Yes | Peer review | No |
| Expert Councils | Yes | Strong | Specialized | Defined technical authority | Limited | Expert review | No |
| Institutional Partners | Yes | Yes | Defined | Partnership-specific | Yes | Program review | No |
| Affected Communities | Yes | Yes | Strong relevance | Defined local/social matters | Participate | Impact review | No |
| Governments | Yes | Yes | Defined participation | Jurisdiction-specific | Yes | Public oversight | No Civizen-wide control |
| Commercial Entity | Defined | Defined | Internal corporate matters | Commercial matters | Yes | Corporate audit | No mission control |
| Investors | Limited | Yes | Corporate rights only | Commercial rights only | No inherent right | Financial oversight where agreed | No mission control |
| AI | Generate | Strong analytical role | No | No | Authorized automation only | Assist | Never |

## 6. Decision Classes

Different classes of decisions should use different governance pathways.

Material **model** changes should use proportional governance: low-impact label/alias changes; moderate Domain/model changes; high-impact score, eligibility, or economic model changes; foundational-purpose changes. See [Shared Classification & Model Evolution Architecture](../03-platform/model-evolution/shared-classification-and-model-evolution-architecture.md). Do not implement approval workflows from this paragraph.

### 6.1 Operational Decisions

Examples:

- staffing;
- routine procurement;
- product implementation;
- scheduling;
- ordinary technical maintenance;
- approved budget execution.

These should generally be delegated to responsible teams.

Broad voting should not be required for routine operational decisions.

### 6.2 Technical Decisions

Examples:

- infrastructure standards;
- data architecture;
- security requirements;
- interoperability;
- technical protocols;
- system reliability.

These should involve qualified technical contributors and relevant Expert Councils.

### 6.3 Professional and Scientific Decisions

Examples:

- health standards;
- educational methodology;
- scientific validation;
- safety protocols;
- research standards.

Qualified expertise must play a substantial role.

Public opinion should not override established evidence without an appropriate evidentiary process.

### 6.4 Program and Pilot Decisions

These may involve:

- implementation partners;
- participating communities;
- relevant experts;
- Civizen program leaders;
- funders;
- and the responsible institutional body.

### 6.5 Economic Decisions

Examples:

- proceeds allocations;
- contributor compensation systems;
- commercial revenue distribution;
- investment arrangements;
- economic incentives.

These require strong transparency, conflict-of-interest protection, financial review, and appropriate approval.

### 6.6 Governance Policy Decisions

These concern the procedures by which Civizen itself operates.

They should generally require broader participation than routine operating decisions.

### 6.7 Constitutional Decisions

These affect Civizen's fundamental mission, rights, safeguards, or institutional identity.

They should receive the highest level of protection.

These seven classes — Operational, Technical, Professional / Scientific, Program / Pilot, Economic, Governance Policy, and Constitutional — are the institutional decision-class model. They must not be collapsed into a single voting mechanism.

A future product implementation may classify proposals along these lines. That engine is **not** implemented by this documentation update. The current application uses a coarser in-app scaffold (`ordinary`, `elevated`, `constitutional`); that scaffold is a product prototype, not a replacement for this Framework.

## 7. Founder Authority and Transition

### 7.1 Formation Stage

During the formation stage, the founder may reasonably exercise substantial authority over:

- institutional design;
- early documentation;
- initial product development;
- recruitment;
- partnership development;
- formation strategy;
- brand stewardship;
- and interpretation of the founding vision.

This concentration of authority is transitional.

### 7.2 Founder Safeguard Role

The founder may retain defined safeguards against actions that would clearly undermine Civizen's founding mission during the early institutional stages.

Such safeguards should not become permanent unilateral control.

### 7.3 Transition

Founder authority should progressively decrease as Civizen develops:

- an independent board;
- credible institutional bodies;
- functioning expert councils;
- a mature contributor base;
- reliable governance technology;
- and legitimate citizen participation.

### 7.4 Founder Cannot Permanently Own Governance

The founder should not retain an unrestricted power to:

- override all votes;
- appoint all future governing bodies indefinitely;
- appropriate Civizen assets;
- privately sell control over the mission;
- or transfer Civizen to another entity without protected approval.

### 7.5 Founder Legacy

The founder's continuing role may evolve into forms such as:

- Founding Steward;
- Founder and Institutional Guardian;
- honorary or advisory roles;
- limited constitutional stewardship;
- or another defined role.

The exact transition is addressed in the [Founder Transition & Succession Framework](./founder-transition-succession-framework.md). Formation-stage principles are also in the [Institutional Blueprint](./institutional-blueprint.md) §§6.2 and 15.

## 8. Foundation Board Authority

The Foundation Board should exercise authority necessary to fulfill the Foundation's lawful fiduciary obligations.

Its responsibilities may include:

- financial oversight;
- asset protection;
- regulatory compliance;
- executive oversight;
- conflict-of-interest enforcement;
- organizational risk;
- safeguarding charitable purposes;
- institutional continuity;
- and approval of legally reserved matters.

The Board should not automatically determine every Civizen governance decision.

Its role should increasingly become one of:

**fiduciary stewardship rather than political ownership.**

The Board should be expected to explain publicly when it cannot implement a governance decision because of:

- law;
- fiduciary duty;
- material safety concerns;
- contractual restrictions;
- or constitutional conflict.

Such refusals should be reviewable.

---

## 9. Citizen Authority

Citizens should progressively receive meaningful authority over matters that legitimately belong to Civizen's wider community.

These may include:

- governance proposals;
- community priorities;
- system-wide policy discussions;
- election or selection of certain representatives;
- review of major institutional directions;
- constitutional participation;
- accountability mechanisms;
- and evaluation of Civizen outcomes.

Citizen governance should not mean unrestricted majority rule.

Safeguards should protect:

- minorities;
- fundamental rights;
- evidence;
- affected groups;
- constitutional principles;
- and long-term system integrity.

---

## 10. Contributor Authority

Contributors should receive increased opportunities for responsibility where their demonstrated contribution and competence justify it.

This may include:

- proposing initiatives;
- reviewing work;
- joining councils;
- leading programs;
- voting in contributor-specific decisions;
- participating in stewardship;
- and becoming eligible for higher governance responsibilities.

Contribution records may support eligibility, but governance should not simply be purchased through contribution volume.

A very large contributor should not automatically receive proportionally greater political power.

---

## 11. Expert Councils

Expert Councils should exist where significant knowledge or professional competence is required.

Their roles may include:

- establishing technical standards;
- evaluating evidence;
- identifying risks;
- reviewing proposals;
- certifying methodologies;
- publishing dissenting opinions;
- recommending actions;
- and rejecting technically invalid proposals within clearly defined mandates.

Expert Councils should be accountable.

They should not become self-perpetuating closed elites.

Council membership should therefore involve:

- transparent qualifications;
- conflicts disclosure;
- defined terms;
- periodic review;
- diversity of perspective;
- and mechanisms for challenge or appeal.

---

## 12. Institutional Partner Participation

Institutional partners may participate according to:

- expertise;
- contribution;
- implementation responsibility;
- affected interests;
- contractual obligations;
- research role;
- funding role;
- and program responsibility.

Institutional participation should not be determined only by organizational category.

A university, for example, may participate in:

- research;
- education;
- health;
- technology;
- governance studies;
- implementation;
- or multiple areas simultaneously.

Institutional representation should not convert organizational power into Civizen-wide control.

---

## 13. Governments and International Organizations

Governments and international organizations can become major Civizen partners.

They may:

- fund;
- implement;
- research;
- host pilots;
- provide public infrastructure;
- regulate;
- participate in standards;
- provide expertise;
- and collaborate in public programs.

No government should gain unilateral governance authority over Civizen merely because it:

- funds a substantial part of the system;
- hosts a major deployment;
- provides legal recognition;
- supplies infrastructure;
- or represents a large population.

Where governmental authority legitimately applies by law, Civizen must respect it.

Where Civizen's internal mission governance is concerned, institutional independence must be preserved.

---

## 14. Commercial Entities and Investors

Commercial activities may require separate governance structures.

Investors may receive rights related to:

- financial reporting;
- corporate voting;
- board representation where applicable;
- investor protections;
- distributions;
- or other commercial matters.

These rights should remain within the legitimate commercial sphere.

Investors should not automatically obtain authority over:

- Civizen's constitutional principles;
- Foundation assets;
- citizen governance;
- non-commercial institutional policy;
- contributor governance;
- or the mission of the broader system.

Commercial success should support Civizen.

It should not become a mechanism for acquiring Civizen.

---

## 15. Artificial Intelligence

AI may become deeply integrated into Civizen governance.

It may assist with:

- evidence gathering;
- proposal analysis;
- scenario modeling;
- detecting contradictions;
- estimating consequences;
- identifying affected populations;
- legal and policy research;
- fraud detection;
- moderation;
- summarization;
- matching expertise;
- detecting conflicts;
- measuring outcomes;
- and monitoring implementation.

AI should identify uncertainty and competing interpretations where relevant.

AI should not possess independent constitutional authority.

AI outputs should remain challengeable and auditable.

Where AI significantly influences a decision, Civizen should preserve sufficient information to allow appropriate human review.

---

## 16. Proposal Process

A mature Civizen proposal process may include:

1. Proposal submission
2. Eligibility review
3. Classification of decision type
4. Identification of affected groups
5. Expert review where needed
6. Evidence collection
7. Public or stakeholder deliberation
8. Amendment
9. Impact assessment
10. Conflict-of-interest review
11. Voting or authorized decision
12. Fiduciary or legal review where applicable
13. Implementation
14. Monitoring
15. Outcome evaluation
16. Retrospective review

Not every proposal requires every stage.

---

## 17. Deliberation

Civizen should prioritize informed deliberation over simple polling.

Governance interfaces should help participants understand:

- what is being proposed;
- why;
- available alternatives;
- supporting evidence;
- costs;
- risks;
- affected populations;
- minority positions;
- expert opinions;
- uncertainty;
- and expected consequences.

Major decisions should preserve a public reasoning record whenever appropriate.

---

## 18. Voting

Different voting mechanisms may be appropriate for different decisions.

Possible mechanisms include:

- simple majority;
- qualified majority;
- supermajority;
- ranked voting;
- representative voting;
- delegated voting;
- stakeholder voting;
- expert approval;
- affected-community consent;
- multi-body approval;
- or combinations of these.

No single voting mechanism should automatically govern all Civizen decisions.

Voting rules must be transparent before the vote occurs.

---

## 19. Reserved Matters

Certain matters should receive extraordinary safeguards.

Reserved Matters may include:

- changing Civizen's fundamental mission;
- dissolution;
- merger;
- transfer of core mission assets;
- transfer of controlling intellectual property;
- permanent abandonment of participatory governance;
- elimination of essential non-discrimination principles;
- conversion of Civizen into exclusively private ownership;
- granting permanent control to a government;
- granting mission control to investors;
- or eliminating constitutional protections against capture.

Reserved Matters should generally require approval from multiple independent sources.

A future model may require combinations such as:

**Foundation Board + constitutional governance body + qualified citizen approval**

or another appropriately distributed process.

---

## 20. Checks and Balances

Civizen should avoid combining:

- proposal;
- approval;
- implementation;
- auditing;
- and appeal

within the same authority for high-impact decisions.

Possible safeguards include:

- independent review;
- public disclosure;
- multiple approvals;
- term limits;
- conflict-of-interest exclusions;
- audit trails;
- minority opinions;
- appeal mechanisms;
- external review;
- and retrospective evaluation.

---

## 21. Conflicts of Interest

Participants should disclose conflicts relevant to decisions in which they participate.

Material conflicts may require:

- disclosure;
- abstention;
- recusal;
- independent review;
- or exclusion from a particular decision.

Conflict rules should apply to:

- founders;
- directors;
- contributors;
- council members;
- institutional partners;
- employees;
- contractors;
- governments;
- commercial entities;
- and investors.

---

## 22. Appeals and Review

Important decisions should have appropriate mechanisms for review.

Possible grounds include:

- procedural error;
- undisclosed conflict;
- material new evidence;
- constitutional violation;
- discriminatory impact;
- technical error;
- fraudulent participation;
- or substantial implementation failure.

Appeal should not mean that every decision can be indefinitely reopened.

Finality rules should exist.

---

## 23. Emergency Authority

Civizen may require temporary emergency authority in cases such as:

- cybersecurity incidents;
- infrastructure failure;
- fraud;
- immediate physical safety risk;
- legal orders;
- financial crisis;
- or other serious threats.

Emergency authority should be:

- narrowly defined;
- temporary;
- documented;
- reviewable;
- proportionate;
- and automatically expire unless properly renewed.

Emergency powers must not become a mechanism for bypassing normal governance indefinitely.

---

## 24. Transparency and Accountability

Civizen should maintain governance records appropriate to the importance of the decision.

These may include:

- proposals;
- votes;
- conflicts;
- expert reviews;
- funding relationships;
- implementation responsibilities;
- amendments;
- outcomes;
- audit findings;
- and appeal decisions.

Some information may require legitimate confidentiality for:

- personal privacy;
- security;
- protected research;
- contractual confidentiality;
- or legal requirements.

Confidentiality should be justified rather than assumed.

---

## 25. Governance Eligibility

Eligibility to exercise particular governance responsibilities may depend on factors such as:

- identity verification where required;
- system standing;
- demonstrated contribution;
- expertise;
- institutional role;
- absence of disqualifying conflicts;
- affected status;
- training;
- past responsibility;
- and relevant experience.

Eligibility should be proportional to the responsibility involved.

Civizen should avoid creating unnecessary barriers to ordinary citizen participation.

---

## 26. Contribution Records and Governance

Civizen should maintain auditable records of meaningful contributions.

This section implements, for governance, the **Contribution Record** principle in the [Institutional Blueprint](./institutional-blueprint.md) §13.2. It does not create a separate contribution scoring methodology.

Contribution records may support:

- recognition;
- compensation;
- proceeds participation;
- institutional history;
- contributor status;
- role eligibility;
- and governance responsibility.

However, contribution metrics should inform governance eligibility rather than mechanically determine governance power.

No single contribution score should become a universal measure of human worth or political authority. A single numeric contribution score must not directly determine voting power.

---

## 27. Progressive Distribution of Governance

Civizen governance should evolve through stages.

### Stage 0 — Founder-Led Formation

Governance is concentrated because the system is still being created.

### Stage 1 — Institutional Governance

Independent board members, policies, institutional partners, and formal accountability mechanisms are established.

### Stage 2 — Structured Participation

Expert Councils, contributor governance, pilot communities, and formal participatory mechanisms expand.

### Stage 3 — Distributed Governance

Substantial decision authority moves into structured multi-stakeholder governance.

### Stage 4 — Mature Governance

Civizen functions through a durable distributed system where no founder, board, country, investor, institution, or technology provider is indispensable to legitimate governance.

---

## 28. Governance Evolution

The Governance Framework itself should be capable of improvement.

Governance mechanisms should be evaluated using evidence such as:

- participation quality;
- decision quality;
- fairness;
- inclusiveness;
- implementation success;
- speed;
- resilience;
- trust;
- abuse resistance;
- expert accuracy;
- citizen satisfaction;
- and long-term consequences.

Governance should not be preserved merely because it was designed first.

Structures should be improved when evidence shows that better arrangements are possible.

## 30. Governance Objective

Civizen governance should ultimately answer a simple question:

> **Who is best positioned and legitimately authorized to make this particular decision, while protecting everyone affected by it and preventing concentrated power from overriding the common interest?**

The answer will not always be the majority.

It will not always be experts.

It will not always be the Board.

It will not always be contributors.

It will not always be governments.

And it should never automatically be whoever provides the most money.

A mature governance system distributes authority according to the nature of the decision, while ensuring that every exercise of power remains accountable to the broader purpose of Civizen.
