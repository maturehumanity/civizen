# Civizen Contributor Framework

**Status: Working Contributor Framework**

**Working Draft 0.1**

This document is a **working institutional design** for how contribution is identified, recorded, evidenced, validated, recognized, and connected to responsibility and opportunity. It is **not** adopted public policy, not a compensation formula, and not a product score specification.

Proposed recording, validation, and opportunity mechanisms remain **proposed** until they exist as operational product features or separately adopted rules. Compensation and economic participation, if any, remain governed by adopted public policy and separately adopted funding instruments.

This Framework is **not** published as a primary `/documents` page and does **not** replace [`contributor-participation-and-recognition.md`](../02-policies/institutional/contributor-participation-and-recognition.md) (`/contribute/policy`).

## 1. Purpose

The Civizen Contributor Framework defines how meaningful contribution to Civizen should be:

- identified;
- recorded;
- attributed;
- evidenced;
- validated;
- evaluated;
- recognized;
- connected to responsibility;
- connected to opportunities;
- and, where appropriate under separately adopted rules, considered for compensation or economic participation.

The Framework applies to contributions made by:

- individuals;
- organizations;
- institutional partners;
- professionals;
- volunteers;
- researchers;
- developers;
- educators;
- public servants;
- students;
- community participants;
- pilot participants;
- businesses;
- governments;
- universities;
- nonprofits;
- and other contributors.

Its purpose is not to convert every human action into a score.

Its purpose is to preserve a credible record of who meaningfully helped build, operate, improve, validate, or expand Civizen and what resulted from that contribution.

## 3. Core Principle

Civizen should recognize meaningful contribution wherever it occurs.

Contribution may come through:

- paid professional work;
- unpaid work;
- volunteering;
- research;
- expertise;
- institutional collaboration;
- technology;
- funding;
- infrastructure;
- knowledge;
- implementation;
- governance;
- review;
- community participation;
- problem-solving;
- innovation;
- mentoring;
- or other forms of meaningful effort.

These forms of contribution are not identical.

They should therefore be recorded distinctly rather than collapsed immediately into one universal number.

---

## 4. Contribution Is Not Human Worth

Civizen must never imply that a person's contribution record represents their value as a human being.

A Contribution Record measures relevant activity within a defined context.

It does not measure:

- dignity;
- moral worth;
- personal value;
- rights;
- human importance;
- or entitlement to basic participation in society.

A person may contribute little to Civizen and still possess exactly the same fundamental human dignity and rights as anyone else.

---

## 5. Contribution Record

Civizen should develop a persistent **Contribution Record**.

The Contribution Record should preserve sufficient information to understand:

- who contributed;
- what they contributed;
- where;
- when;
- through which project or institution;
- under which role;
- whether the activity was paid or unpaid;
- what evidence exists;
- who validated it;
- what responsibility was assumed;
- what outcome resulted;
- and what impact can reasonably be attributed.

The Contribution Record should become an institutional memory system rather than simply an activity counter.

This Framework is the canonical detailed source for Contribution Record design. Other institutional documents should link here rather than repeating the full field list.

---

## 6. Contribution Record Structure

A contribution entry may eventually include fields such as:

- contributor;
- contributor type;
- field/domain;
- organization or affiliation;
- pilot/program/project;
- role;
- task or activity;
- contribution category;
- description;
- start date;
- end date;
- duration;
- paid/unpaid status;
- contractual scope;
- additional contribution beyond paid scope;
- evidence;
- validator;
- validation status;
- quality;
- responsibility;
- difficulty;
- uniqueness;
- measurable outcome;
- impact;
- beneficiaries;
- dependencies;
- collaborators;
- attribution share;
- conflicts;
- recognition;
- and later compensation status where applicable.

Not every contribution requires every field.

## 7. Contributor Types

Civizen should distinguish at least between:

### Individual Contributors

People contributing personally.

### Institutional Contributors

Organizations contributing resources, infrastructure, funding, expertise, personnel, access, or implementation capacity.

### Team Contributors

Groups whose work cannot reasonably be attributed only to individuals.

### Partner Contributors

Individuals or institutions contributing through an established partnership.

### Independent Contributors

People contributing without institutional affiliation.

One contribution may involve several contributor types simultaneously.

## 9. Paid Work

Paid work is still contribution.

Civizen should record meaningful paid work where useful to institutional history and accountability.

However, paid work must be clearly labeled as paid.

Receiving payment does not erase contribution.

At the same time, normal fulfillment of a paid obligation should not automatically create an additional future compensation claim.

---

## 10. Contribution Beyond Paid Scope

A person may contribute beyond what they were:

- hired;
- contracted;
- assigned;
- funded;
- or institutionally required

to do.

Such additional work may be separately recorded when it is:

- identifiable;
- meaningful;
- outside the normal compensated scope;
- and reasonably evidenced.

Example:

A university researcher assigned to evaluate one Civizen pilot may independently develop a methodology later adopted across multiple Civizen programs.

The person's institutionally funded work and additional contribution should be capable of separate attribution.

---

## 11. Institutional vs Individual Contribution

When work occurs through a partner organization, Civizen should avoid attributing everything solely to the institution.

Where reasonably possible, contribution records should distinguish:

**Institutional contribution**

from:

**individual contribution within that institution.**

For example:

A university may provide:

- laboratory facilities;
- research funding;
- administrative support;
- and institutional authorization.

Individual researchers may separately contribute:

- methodologies;
- analysis;
- discoveries;
- leadership;
- or work beyond funded responsibilities.

Both levels matter.

---

## 12. Team Contribution

Many outcomes will result from collaboration.

Civizen should avoid creating artificial individual competition by pretending that every outcome can be precisely divided.

Team contribution records may therefore identify:

- team;
- participants;
- roles;
- shared outcome;
- and individual contributions where reasonably identifiable.

Not every contribution requires exact percentage attribution.

---

## 13. Contribution Evidence

Important contribution claims should be supported by evidence appropriate to the activity.

Possible evidence includes:

- completed work;
- source-code history;
- documents;
- research;
- project records;
- task completion;
- institutional confirmation;
- participant feedback;
- measurable outcomes;
- peer review;
- audit records;
- publications;
- contracts;
- or other verifiable artifacts.

Evidence requirements should be proportional to the significance of the contribution.

---

## 14. Contribution Validation

Contribution may be validated through different mechanisms.

These may include:

- automated verification;
- project lead confirmation;
- peer review;
- institutional verification;
- expert review;
- participant feedback;
- governance review;
- objective system records;
- or combinations of these.

Self-reported contribution may be useful but should not automatically receive the same confidence as independently verified contribution.

---

## 15. Validation Status

Contribution records may eventually have statuses such as:

- Recorded
- Pending Verification
- Verified
- Disputed
- Revised
- Rejected
- Archived

These statuses describe confidence in the record rather than the human value of the contributor.

---

## 16. Contribution Quality

Quantity alone should not determine contribution importance.

Where appropriate, Civizen may consider qualities such as:

- accuracy;
- usefulness;
- reliability;
- professionalism;
- originality;
- completeness;
- durability;
- efficiency;
- safety;
- collaboration;
- and participant outcomes.

---

## 17. Responsibility

Contribution may also differ according to responsibility assumed.

For example:

- completing a simple task;
- leading a complex project;
- being accountable for participant safety;
- designing a critical architecture;
- managing significant resources;
- or accepting institutional responsibility

represent different levels of responsibility.

Responsibility may be recorded separately from time or quantity.

---

## 18. Difficulty and Scarcity

Some contributions require:

- rare expertise;
- unusual effort;
- significant risk;
- difficult conditions;
- specialized credentials;
- or substantial opportunity cost.

These factors may matter when evaluating contribution.

They should not automatically override actual outcomes.

---

## 19. Impact

Civizen should attempt to understand what happened because of a contribution.

Possible impact indicators include:

- people helped;
- quality improved;
- cost saved;
- time saved;
- risk reduced;
- learning improved;
- participation increased;
- technology enabled;
- systems created;
- institutional adoption;
- environmental benefit;
- partnerships created;
- funding secured;
- or other measurable outcomes.

Impact measurement should acknowledge uncertainty.

---

## 20. Attribution

Impact is often shared.

Civizen should avoid falsely attributing an entire outcome to one contributor when many people or institutions were necessary.

Contribution records should support:

- shared attribution;
- dependencies;
- collaborative outcomes;
- and reasonable uncertainty.

---

## 21. Contribution and Civizen Score

The Contribution Record and Civizen Score should not be identical systems.

The Contribution Record contains detailed historical evidence.

The Civizen Score may summarize selected aspects of participation or standing.

A summary score should not replace the underlying record.

Likewise, one number should not determine:

- compensation;
- governance power;
- access to every feature;
- institutional authority;
- or human worth.

---

## 22. Contribution and Reputation

Verified contribution may appropriately influence reputation.

However, reputation should be contextual.

Someone may have strong:

- medical contribution;
- software contribution;
- education contribution;
- community contribution;
- or governance contribution

without that automatically establishing competence in unrelated areas.

Civizen should increasingly support domain-specific reputation rather than assuming universal expertise.

---

## 23. Contribution and Field / Domain

Every meaningful contribution should be capable of association with one or more relevant fields.

Examples:

- Health
- Education
- Culture
- Governance
- Environment
- Technology
- AI
- Economics
- Employment
- Law
- Research
- Community Development

This allows Civizen to understand where a person's contribution and experience actually exist.

---

## 24. Contribution and Skills

Contribution records may provide evidence that a contributor has used or developed particular skills.

They should not automatically replace:

- education;
- professional credentials;
- licenses;
- certifications;
- or independent skills verification.

Contribution may complement those sources.

---

## 25. Contribution and Experience

Verified contribution can become part of a person's demonstrated experience.

This may eventually help Civizen understand:

- what someone has actually done;
- under what responsibility;
- with what outcome;
- and in which context.

This can strengthen Civizen's future work and role-matching systems.

---

## 26. Contribution and Responsibility Eligibility

Meaningful contribution may support eligibility for greater responsibility.

For example, sustained verified contribution may help qualify someone to:

- lead a pilot;
- join a working group;
- review proposals;
- serve on a council;
- mentor contributors;
- manage projects;
- or become eligible for defined governance roles.

Contribution alone should not guarantee appointment.

Other requirements may include:

- expertise;
- integrity;
- identity verification;
- conflicts review;
- training;
- institutional mandate;
- or election/selection.

---

## 27. Contribution and Governance

Civizen should preserve the distinction:

> **Contribution can support governance eligibility without mechanically determining governance power.**

Someone who has demonstrated significant contribution to Education may reasonably become more eligible to participate in Education-related governance.

That does not imply that the person should receive ten votes because another person receives one.

---

## 28. Contribution and Access

Contribution history may be one factor in determining whether someone should receive access to:

- working groups;
- unfinished areas;
- expert materials;
- review processes;
- sensitive pilot work;
- or higher-responsibility tasks.

Access should follow the broader Civizen principle:

**Need + Role + Relevance + Responsibility + Authorization**

Contribution is only one component.

---

## 29. Contribution and Opportunities

The Contribution Record should eventually help Civizen recommend:

- tasks;
- projects;
- pilots;
- leadership roles;
- research;
- professional opportunities;
- mentorship;
- partnership work;
- or other ways a person can contribute meaningfully.

The objective is not only to record the past.

It is to help people find where they can be most useful next.

---

## 30. Contribution and Recognition

Civizen may recognize contribution through mechanisms such as:

- public attribution;
- contributor profiles;
- verified accomplishments;
- roles;
- acknowledgements;
- professional records;
- project credits;
- institutional recognition;
- badges or distinctions where useful;
- or historical records.

Recognition should reflect real contribution rather than gamified activity accumulation.

---

## 31. Contribution and Compensation

Some contribution may qualify for compensation.

Compensation may take forms such as:

- wages;
- contracts;
- honoraria;
- grants;
- prizes;
- reimbursements;
- revenue participation;
- proceeds allocation;
- or other approved mechanisms.

The Contribution Record provides evidence.

It does not itself create an automatic right to payment.

---

## 32. Future Economic Participation

The Funding Constitution may establish economic allocations for:

- investors;
- contributors;
- institutional partners;
- or other qualifying participants.

Where private investment is unnecessary because Civizen becomes substantially funded and developed through public-interest or non-commercial support, some allocation that would otherwise compensate investors may potentially become available to contributors or partners.

Eligibility and allocation must depend on separately adopted rules.

The Contributor Framework should support those rules with evidence.

---

## 33. No Retroactive Guessing

One reason the Contribution Record should begin early is to avoid trying years later to reconstruct who contributed what.

Civizen should preserve meaningful contribution history while the evidence is available.

Later compensation or recognition systems can then evaluate real records rather than relying primarily on memory or personal influence.

---

## 34. No Automatic Promise

Recording contribution must not be presented as:

> "Do this now and Civizen promises to pay you later."

Unless an explicit contractual or adopted compensation arrangement exists, contribution records establish attribution and evidence, not guaranteed future payment.

Public contribution interfaces should communicate this clearly.

---

## 35. Volunteer Contribution

Volunteer contribution should remain genuinely voluntary.

Civizen should avoid implying guaranteed future economic compensation merely to encourage unpaid work.

Where compensation possibilities exist, they should be described accurately.

Volunteers should receive appropriate recognition and contribution records even where no financial compensation occurs.

---

## 36. Professional Contribution

Professionals should be able to offer:

- expertise;
- consulting;
- technical work;
- research;
- review;
- mentoring;
- project leadership;
- or other professional services.

Civizen should distinguish between:

- paid professional engagements;
- pro bono professional work;
- voluntary professional contribution;
- and institutional contribution performed through an employer or partner.

---

## 37. Financial Contribution

Financial support should be recorded separately from labor or professional contribution.

Types may include:

- donation;
- grant;
- sponsorship;
- investment;
- program funding;
- public funding;
- institutional contribution;
- or another appropriate category.

Financial contribution should not automatically increase civic governance authority.

---

## 38. Knowledge Contribution

Civizen should provide mechanisms through which users may contribute:

- information;
- research;
- educational resources;
- professional knowledge;
- methods;
- evidence;
- translations;
- documentation;
- or other useful knowledge.

Knowledge contribution should be subject to:

- provenance;
- quality;
- intellectual-property;
- accuracy;
- and review requirements

where relevant.

---

## 39. Improvement Contribution

People should be able to suggest improvements to Civizen itself.

Useful improvement contributions may include:

- bug reports;
- feature proposals;
- policy improvements;
- governance improvements;
- usability findings;
- security disclosures;
- process improvements;
- or architectural suggestions.

The value of an improvement should depend on usefulness and impact rather than merely submission volume.

---

## 40. Task Contribution

Civizen should eventually support open and assigned tasks.

Tasks may be:

- public;
- domain-specific;
- pilot-specific;
- organization-specific;
- skill-specific;
- restricted;
- or invitation-only.

Task completion should create contribution evidence when appropriate.

---

## 41. Community Contribution

Community projects and challenges may create meaningful contribution opportunities.

Civizen should distinguish genuine community benefit from participation designed only to accumulate score.

Impact and quality should matter more than activity count.

---

## 42. Contribution Opportunities

A mature Civizen system should be able to publish contribution opportunities containing fields such as:

- title;
- area;
- pilot;
- organization;
- problem;
- required skills;
- required expertise;
- estimated effort;
- location;
- remote/on-site;
- role;
- compensation status;
- access level;
- deadline;
- responsible person;
- expected outcome;
- and contribution-record treatment.

---

## 43. Personalized Contribution Discovery

Contribution opportunities should increasingly be personalized according to:

- field;
- skills;
- experience;
- location;
- organization affiliation;
- pilot membership;
- role;
- prior contribution;
- availability;
- authorization;
- and interests.

The objective is to show people relevant ways to help rather than overwhelming them with every possible task.

---
